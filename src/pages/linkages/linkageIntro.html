Introduction

A mechanism is a collection of links and joints (linkage) designed in such a way as to create a desired motion output. One link of a mechanism is grounded, that is, fixed to some reference frame, and we generally wish to find the motion of the remaining links. Some examples of mechanisms are windshield wiper blades, the crankshaft/connecting rod/piston assembly in a car engine, door hinges, mechanical watches and clocks, robotic manipulators, etc.

In designing a mechanism, it is often critical to know its mobility, or the number of degrees of freedom it possesses. Some mechanisms have so many links and joints that it is difficult to determine at a glance whether they are capable of movement at all. To determine the mobility of a mechanism, we define the number of degrees of freedom (DOF) as "The number of independent coordinates needed to completely define an object's position and orientation in space."

In this introductory book, we limit mechanism analysis to two-dimensional space and propose that most practical mechanisms have exactly one grounded link or a fixed link. In the case where the entire mechanism is moving, as in the engine of a car, we choose one link (usually the engine block) to be ground, and analyze the movement of the remaining links relative to this. This fixed link can assume any shape necessary to fix all the fixed pivots to the grounded link.

Using these assumptions we may use the modified Gruebler's equation to determine the mobility of any two-dimensional linkage:

DOF = 3(L − 1) − 2(Jp + Jfs) − Jhs

Where

  • L = Number of links
  • Jp = Number of pins
  • Jfs = Number of full-sliders
  • Jhs = Number of half-sliders
A full explanation of how each type of connection reduces the degrees of freedom can be found in the textbook.
